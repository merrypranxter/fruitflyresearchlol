Where the design space gets stranger
The first hundred ideas still mostly describe an organism acting on a world.
The more radical step is to let its actions change the division between organism and world.
A useful research precedent is that soft-body dynamics can contribute memory and nonlinear
computation, not merely execute commands. [2]
Proposed implication: a body, a material field, or a structure built by the organism could
become part of its effective controller. That does not make every sculpture intelligent; it gives a
precise experimental question:
         Does removing this structure remove a capability, even though the neural simulation
         and learned decoder remain unchanged?

The important control distinction
A few neural channels can select among rich behaviors without independently commanding
every actuator.
But three claims must remain separate:
 1. Selection: the neural state chooses a behavior from a library.
 2. Modulation: it changes the strength, timing, or location of an autonomous process.
 3. Synthesis: it generates the fine structure of the action itself.
For your work, selection and modulation are not disappointing compromises. They may be the
most productive interfaces—provided the action library is not secretly doing all the decision-
making.
Consider a crystal explosion:
     Neural decision: release here, now, with this much precursor and this directional bias.
     Body mechanism: open a valve and transfer stored material.
     World mechanism: grow branches according to local conditions.
     Feedback: detect new boundaries, resource depletion, blockage, and illumination.
     Learning: change future release decisions based on those consequences.
That is a consequential organism even though no neuron individually specifies each crystal
facet.
The weak version would be: neural activity crosses a threshold, and a canned crystal animation
plays regardless of conditions.


Give the system a scientific spine
Before adding many more capabilities, make every proposed organism answer the same small
set of questions.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 8 / 44>
A capability contract
Each sensor should declare:

  What quantity is measured?
  Where is it measured?
  What spatial or temporal window is used?
  How is it normalized?
  Which neural cells receive the resulting input?
  Which parts of this mapping are biological, inferred, or invented?


Each action should declare:

  Which neural measurements influence it?
  What additional controller state influences it?
  What can the action change?
  What does it cost?
  What limits or stabilizers modify it?
  How does its consequence return through the sensors?


Each learning mechanism should declare:

  What parameters change?
  Which signal causes the update?
  What information is unavailable to the learner?
  What is saved?
  What is reset between trials?
  What evaluation runs without learning?


This is not bureaucratic overhead. It lets an artwork remain mysterious in appearance without
becoming mysterious in explanation.

A contribution ledger
For every issued action, record the proposed fields:

  simulation time
  sensor observations
  stimulated neural populations
  measured neural features
  decoder outputs
  engineered reflex corrections
  exploration contribution
  final bounded action
  resource cost
  subsequent measured consequence


Avoid trying to assign a single percentage such as “73% fly-controlled” to a nonlinear hybrid.
Instead ask intervention-specific questions:
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 9 / 44>
     Does removing neural input change action selection?
     Does replaying another organism’s neural activity change its trajectory?
     Can the engineered controller solve the task alone?
     Does the trained decoder use neural features or ignore them?
     Does biological topology help after alternatives receive equal training?
Those are answerable.

Two different kinds of proof
Dependence test: keep the trained decoder fixed, then interrupt or replace its neural inputs.
This tests whether the existing organism depends on those inputs.
Utility test: independently train matched decoders on biological, rewired, random, and non-
neural representations.
This tests whether biological structure is useful relative to alternatives.
A system can pass the first and fail the second. Its decoder may genuinely depend on a
biological network without that network being a particularly good substrate.
That outcome would still teach you something.

These are different mechanisms, not interchangeable names for curiosity.

For example:
    Novelty can favor unfamiliar outcomes.
    Learning progress can favor outcomes becoming more controllable.
    Homeostasis can favor restoring an internal resource.
    A diversity archive can preserve alternatives that a single reward would discard.
The proposed organism could use all four, but you should log their contributions separately.

An exploration architecture
Instead of one scalar called taste, consider four layers:

  INITIAL PRIOR
  Your starting aesthetic biases:
  saturation, contour structure, sparkle, branching, etc.

  VIABILITY
  Material reserves, damage, action costs, repair needs.

  LEARNED COMPETENCE
  Which interventions currently work,
  under which conditions,
  with what predictable consequences?

  DEVELOPMENTAL INTEREST
  Which achievable outcomes are worth practicing next?


A goal might be:
       Create a branching structure that remains traversable and does not exhaust the crystal
       reservoir.
Another:
       Move pigment into a protected cavity using an obstacle rather than direct deposition.
Another:
       Produce an optical effect that remains visible after the organism moves away.
These goals concern capabilities and consequences, not just appearances.
A system that eventually chooses branching over sparkle because branching enables storage,
movement, or new sensing is more interesting than one whose sparkle coefficient slowly
decreases on a timer.


The 20 most interesting untried directions
“Untried” here is a research shortlist, not a verified priority claim. These are proposed
combinations whose exact fly-connectome implementations were not established in the
reviewed evidence. Some have close precedents in artificial life, robotics, or machine learning.
The question for each is not “can a shader display this?” It is “can this become a consequential
part of an organism’s sensorimotor organization?”

1. Grow an external nervous system
Mechanism: the organism lays conductive-looking filaments through its world. When a
filament connects two sites, it creates an explicitly artificial delayed sensor–actuator pathway.
The original connectome stays unchanged. The organism builds additional environment-
mediated feedback around it.
Why it is different: construction changes the architecture through which future consequences
reach the controller.
First test: let it connect a remote pigment sensor to a local indicator. Compare intact, severed,
and decorative-only filaments.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 12 / 44>
Boundary: calling the filaments “nerves” is a metaphor unless their signaling dynamics are
actually modeled.

2. Build its own retina
Mechanism: the organism moves lenses, occluders, or refractive material in front of a fixed
sensor array.
It cannot directly increase sensor resolution. It must improve observations by modifying the
optics.
Why it is different: a structure is both artwork and perceptual apparatus.
First test: two visually ambiguous resource patches become distinguishable only after a lens is
positioned correctly.
Boundary: the learner must receive the resulting sensor measurements—not a hidden flag
saying which patch is nutritious.

3. Outsource a reflex into matter
Mechanism: an initially active controller repeatedly corrects a body’s response. A slower
morphology optimizer changes stiffness or geometry until some correction becomes
mechanically unnecessary.
Why it is different: competence migrates from active control into body structure.
First test: a ribbon learns—or is optimized—to recoil from contact with fewer active corrections.
Measurement: preserve task performance while reducing corrective actuation.
Boundary: morphology optimization is an artificial learning process; the connectome should
not receive credit for inventing the optimizer.

4. Define a body by control
Mechanism: periodically estimate which world variables the organism can reliably influence
and sense over a chosen horizon.
Treat that changing set as its operational body.
Why it is different: a reservoir, distant gate, or cultivated organism can become part of the body
without being physically attached.
First test: give an agent control of a remote valve, then disconnect the channel. Measure
whether its useful control territory expands and contracts.
Boundary: this is an operational definition, not a discovery of the organism’s subjective self.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 13 / 44>
5. Plant future laws
Mechanism: the organism can place expensive “rule seeds.” After a delay or environmental
trigger, each seed changes one local interaction rule.
For example, a region that currently consumes pigment later becomes a crystal-growth
inhibitor.
Why it is different: an action changes the conditions under which later actions will matter.
First test: choose between an immediate pigment deposit and a delayed conversion patch.
Boundary: allow only a small rule grammar; arbitrary code execution is unnecessary and
makes evaluation much harder.

6. Migrate between dimensions
Mechanism: preserve a common local sensor/action vocabulary while moving an organism
among a graph, a two-dimensional field, and a three-dimensional volume.
“Forward,” “contact,” and “resource nearby” must be redefined through documented adapters.
Why it is different: adaptation concerns the structure of space, not simply another animal
mesh.
First test: transfer a two-dimensional navigation policy to a graph with matched local
branching choices.
Boundary: successful transfer may belong to invariant adapters rather than the neural graph;
compare both.

7. Make the world an address bus
Mechanism: the organism sends a material packet down a channel. Its destination determines
which remote actuator receives a later command.
Why it is different: physical routing selects where action occurs; the controller does not get a
free global address.
First test: route one colored packet to either a growth valve or a pigment valve.
Artistic result: luminous canals become visible control infrastructure.
Boundary: transport delays and routing errors must affect behavior rather than remain
decorative.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 14 / 44>
8. Share a skin, not a goal
Mechanism: two controllers inhabit one membrane but maintain different internal resources
and objectives.
One needs exposure to light. The other needs protected cavities. Both act on the same
geometry.
Why it is different: cooperation is not preassigned; anatomy itself is contested.
First test: two regions of a deformable strip share a limited bending budget.
Measurement: identify stable compromises, oscillatory conflict, and collapse.

9. Reproduce construction programs
Mechanism: the organism deposits a compact executable growth grammar, not merely a visual
copy of its body.
The offspring’s form depends on local material and encounters.
Why it is different: reproduction transfers a developmental procedure.
First test: inherit one branching rule and one resource preference while randomizing the
environment.
Boundary: inherited growth code is not inherited memory unless memory state is explicitly
copied.

10. Recognize its work without labels
Mechanism: remove all provenance metadata from the controller’s observations. Mix its
deposits with visually matched foreign deposits.
Why it is different: “my earlier creation” becomes a perceptual and historical inference rather
than a privileged tag.
First test: ask whether it preferentially repairs structures associated with its own past action
sequences.
Measurement: compare true history, swapped history, and appearance-matched decoys.
Boundary: successful discrimination would not establish self-awareness.

11. Budget time as anatomy
Mechanism: the organism allocates a limited number of update events among tissue processes.
It might keep a sensor patch fast while allowing a crystal organ to evolve slowly.
Why it is different: temporal resolution becomes a resource the organism manages.
First test: divide a fixed update budget between sensing and growth.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 15 / 44>
Boundary: changing update frequency can introduce numerical artifacts. The world must
specify whether this represents fictional reaction kinetics, computational scheduling, or both.

12. Eat causal power
Mechanism: reward reproducible influence over future observations rather than raw visual
change.
An action that generates unpredictable static should not automatically be valuable.
Why it is different: the organism seeks environments where it can make meaningful
distinctions happen.
First test: offer a noisy glitter fountain and a quieter controllable wave chamber.
Boundary: a practical influence score is not automatically a valid estimate of empowerment;
describe the approximation.

13. Repair the feedback loop
Mechanism: environmental damage interrupts a useful sensory pathway. The organism can
rebuild the physical connection.
Why it is different: repair restores a cognitive capability, not merely appearance or locomotion.
First test: cut a channel carrying material to a remote sensor and allow the organism to
reconnect it.
Measurement: compare useful sensing before damage, after damage, and after repair.

14. Negotiate sensory contracts
Mechanism: two organisms exchange signals, but each initially interprets them differently.
Their adapters co-adapt while solving a cooperative problem.
Why it is different: communication requires developing a shared mapping rather than
transmitting already meaningful labels.
First test: one agent knows resource identity; the other controls access. Permit only a few
discrete signals.
Boundary: learned signaling is not evidence that the biological networks acquired language.

15. Build tools that alter interactions
Mechanism: a constructed object changes how other materials interact—for example, a
membrane lets solvent pass while blocking pigment.
Why it is different: a tool modifies a relationship, not merely applies force.
First test: build a filter that makes a previously unusable resource accessible.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 16 / 44>
Artistic result: delicate meshes and membranes become functional chemistry.

16. Inherit an ancestor’s unfinished problem
Mechanism: each generation receives persistent structures, depleted resources, and dormant
seeds, but not the previous policy.
Why it is different: the inherited developmental challenge changes even when the agent starts
fresh.
First test: compare descendants in ancestral gardens against identical descendants in reset
gardens.
Measurement: determine whether environmental inheritance improves competence, traps
behavior, or creates new niches.

17. Grow out of a sense
Mechanism: an early sensor gradually becomes less informative while a newly developed organ
offers a different channel.
Why it is different: adaptation includes developmental sensory succession.
First test: slowly reduce a direct resource-concentration channel while allowing an optical or
contact-based alternative.
Boundary: do not interpret forced sensor loss as spontaneous preference evolution.

18. Pay for control infrastructure
Mechanism: sensing, communication, memory maintenance, and actuation all consume
explicit artificial resources.
Why it is different: the organism must decide how much controller it can afford.
First test: choose between a cheap local sensor and an expensive remote sensor that accesses
richer information.
Measurement: resource-adjusted performance and infrastructure persistence.
Boundary: simulated resource cost is a model choice, not a measurement of actual computer
electricity consumption.

19. Branch biographies, not just random seeds
Mechanism: fork one complete checkpoint into several futures, then later expose all
descendants to identical probes.
Why it is different: divergent histories become experimentally attributable.
First test: one childhood rewards delayed construction; another favors immediate
consumption.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 17 / 44>
Measurement: which differences remain after internal reserves, current surroundings, and
temporary satiation are equalized?
Boundary: compare retained learning, not merely different current states.

20. Cultivate dynamics it cannot command
Mechanism: the organism can feed, shelter, or redirect an autonomous pattern but cannot
directly set its detailed state.
Why it is different: successful control means maintaining a productive relationship with
something partly uncontrollable.
First test: cultivate a moving excitable pattern whose exact trajectory is not directly
addressable.
Artistic result: the organism becomes a gardener of alien activity rather than its omnipotent
animator.
Boundary: unpredictability alone is not autonomy. The cultivated process needs persistent
state and its own specified dynamics.

The question

encountered a feature to support useful material behavior?
This is deliberately narrower than asking whether it understands contours.

      One moving organism.
      Two material patches.

      Persistent contour boundaries.
      A bounded movement cost.

          Reach and graze one available patch without repeatedly colliding with its boundary.
Do not initially reward beauty, novelty, symmetry, and survival simultaneously.

      Distance to the nearest relevant boundary.
      Change in that distance.
      Available pigment concentration.
      Optional local curvature, introduced only after the basic test works.

The question
Can an organism do more than trigger effects?
Specifically:
       Can its actions move material through a persistent cycle in which consumption, storage,
       conversion, and release change what it can do next?

The world

Internal state

  energy
  damage
  ingestion cooldown
  extrusion cooldown

3. Eight-Arm Stirrer

The question
Can the current neural substrate contribute useful coordination to an eight-arm body when the
body supplies local mechanics?
Do not begin with locomotion, dexterous grasping, three-dimensional self-collision, and an
accurate octopus muscle model.
Begin with stirring.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 25 / 44>
The body
Use a fixed or slowly moving central hub with eight planar elastic arms.
A starting configuration could use:
    Eight arms.
    A small chain of nodes per arm.
    Stretch and bend limits.
    Damping.
    Contact with pigment or movable particles.
    Optional tip adhesion.
These are prototype choices, not an anatomical octopus model.
Each arm receives local sensory information:
    Contact.
    Curvature.
    Strain.
    Tip position relative to the hub.

Local controllers
Each arm can implement:
    Passive return toward rest.
    Contact-limited contraction.
    A withdrawal reflex.
    Optional oscillation.
    Maximum-force and curvature limits.
Every such mechanism must be logged as engineered.
The central neural system does not deserve credit for an arm retracting if a local hard-coded
reflex caused it.

Central neural interface
First try a synergy interface:
    Global extension.
    Global contraction.
    Left/right or angular bias.
    Stirring intensity.

4. Pigment Notebook

The question
Can an organism construct an environmental trace and later use it as memory?
This is more revealing than simply adding a larger internal memory array.

The world
Build a small arena with:
    A cue location.
    A delay or intervening journey.

    A later choice between two gates.
    A pigment the organism can deposit and sense.
The cue tells it which gate will later be useful.
The cue disappears before the choice.

Two versions
Version A: communication through time
The organism leaves a mark near the eventual decision point while the cue is available, then
later encounters it.
Version B: constructed routing
The organism changes a channel or barrier so that, later, local flow provides the correct signal.
Version B is especially interesting because the memory is not merely a colored token. It is a
changed causal arrangement.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 29 / 44>
Neural interface
Use verified sensory inputs for local mark detection through an explicitly artificial encoder.
The readout chooses:
    Whether to mark.
    Where to mark.
    Which mark type, if more than one exists.
    Which gate to approach.
Begin with a frozen neural graph.

The hidden-memory audit
This experiment fails scientifically if an unnoticed variable already stores the answer.
Audit:
    Decoder recurrent state.
    Feature smoothing.
    Previous action.
    Position history.
    Random-number state correlations.
    Episode metadata.
    Object identifiers.
    Hidden provenance tags.
    Training-state updates during evaluation.
The organism should not receive correctGate through an accidental side channel.

Decisive interventions
Use a small factorial experiment:

            Neural/controller transient   Environmental trace                     Purpose
            Preserved                     Preserved             Full system

            Reset                         Preserved             Can the environment carry the information?

            Preserved                     Erased                Can internal state carry it instead?

            Reset                         Erased                Neither proposed memory source remains

Also:
    Swap the marks between gates.
    Replace them with appearance-matched decoys.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 30 / 44>
    Preserve marks visually but disable their sensor effect.
    Preserve sensor effects while rendering them invisibly to the human viewer.
Those interventions separate appearance, sensing, and information storage.

Measurements
    Correct later choices.
    Performance as delay increases.
    Dependence on trace location.
    Dependence on mark identity.
    Recovery after transient-state reset.
    Reversal when marks are swapped.
    Construction cost.

Expected failures
    It follows a fixed route that happens to be correct.
    The mark never affects the sensor.
    The correct answer remains in a hidden variable.
    It learns “always mark left.”
    A visual trace is present but unused.
    The neural circuit contributes nothing beyond a threshold relay.

First completion criterion
After transient neural/controller state is reset, an intact trace should preserve useful
performance; erasing or swapping it should predictably change the later choice.
This would establish external-memory use by the hybrid system.
It would not establish that the biological graph invented memory or possesses a human-like
concept of writing.


5. Twin Taste Histories

The question
Can two identical starting organisms develop retained, experience-dependent behavioral
differences—rather than merely displaying different current hunger levels or random
coefficients?
This is the experiment closest to your wish that an organism begin with your tastes and
eventually become something you did not completely prescribe.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 31 / 44>
Initial conditions
Create two copies with identical:
    Connectome.
    Neural parameters.
    Encoder.
    Decoder initialization.
    Drive equations.
    Initial aesthetic prior.
    Internal reserves.
    Learning rule.
Save a complete initial checkpoint.
Then expose them to different developmental histories.

The worlds
Offer two intervention families:
    Structured sparkle/crystal production.
    Branching or filament construction.
Both should have:
    Several possible sites.
    Comparable action costs.
    Persistent consequences.
    Opportunities for improvement.
    More than one successful strategy.
Do not make sparkle trivially cheap and branching prohibitively expensive unless cost is the
variable under study.

Developmental histories
Example:
Twin A: early environments make branching useful for accessing resources and shelter.
Twin B: early environments make carefully placed sparkle structures useful for guiding flow or
exposing resources.
The learning rule and viability objective remain the same.
The environments differ in what works.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 32 / 44>
Learning architecture
Keep three quantities separate:
  1. Current internal state: reserves, damage, satiation.
  2. Learned action value or competence: what the organism expects particular actions to
     accomplish.
  3. Longer-term preference parameters: only if you deliberately include a mechanism that
     changes them.
A learned tendency to choose branching can arise from item 2 without item 3 changing at all.
That is still meaningful adaptation. It just should not be mislabeled as an entirely new intrinsic
value.

A practical first learner
Use your neural measurements as part of a small action-value or policy readout.
Maintain an experience archive containing:

  observed context
  neural features
  selected intervention
  material cost
  immediate outcome
  delayed outcome
  learned value update


Begin with a fixed feature vocabulary.
Learned perceptual embeddings and self-generated goals can come later, once the simpler
experiment is interpretable.

Neutral probes
After development:
    Freeze learning.
    Equalize reserves.
    Reset temporary satiation.
    Use the same arena.
    Offer matched opportunities.
    Randomize left/right placement.
    Change superficial colors while preserving mechanics.
    Change mechanics while preserving appearance.
Ask which preferences persist.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 33 / 44>
This separates:
    Color association.
    Motor habit.
    Resource strategy.
    Temporary internal state.
    Retained learned value.
    Any deliberately adaptive aesthetic parameters.

State swaps
Now perform the interesting intervention:
    Swap learned decoders.
    Swap only preference parameters.
    Swap only internal reserves.
    Swap neural transient states.
    Swap environmental histories while leaving controllers untouched.
Which swap carries the behavioral difference?
That is where the organism’s individuality is implemented.

Controls
    Frozen learner.
    Shuffled reward.
    Random exploration with no learning.
    Identical histories.
    Different histories with identical total resource exposure.
    Raw-sensor controller with an equal learning budget.
    Matched rewired neural substrate.
Use several independent history pairs; one dramatic pair is an artwork, not yet a robust result.

Measurements
    Choice proportions under neutral probes.
    Retention after restart.
    Transfer to new layouts.
    Sensitivity to cost changes.
    Reversal under changed contingencies.
    Within-agent stability.
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 34 / 44>
    Between-agent divergence.
    Which saved-state components explain the divergence.

Expected failures
    “Different tastes” disappear after reserves are equalized.
    Random drift produces differences unrelated to experience.
    The learner memorizes location.
    One intervention has a hidden cost advantage.
    The aesthetic feature detector rewards a rendering artifact.
    The connectome is ignored by the learned policy.
    A claimed new preference is simply the original reward function operating correctly in a
    new context.

First completion criterion
The twins should develop reproducible differences that:
    Depend on their histories.
    Persist with learning frozen.
    Survive equalized temporary state.
    Generalize beyond the training scene.
    Move with an identifiable learned-state component during swaps.
That is the point where “it has its own taste” becomes more than a poetic description: not
proof of subjective desire, but a precise, testable claim about an organism whose experience
changed what it chooses to make.
